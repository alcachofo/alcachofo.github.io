**CTF:** ECSC 2026 Spanish Qualifier (INCIBE)  
**Category:** Misc  
**Difficulty:** 900  

## Challenge

One file: `video.avi`. 256x128, 55 frames, 10 fps. The statement described a screen crossed by
several oscillating beams, only one of which carried the intercepted beacon transmission.

## Solution

### What is actually on screen

Watching it back tells you nothing. It is black with a scatter of white dots that reshuffles
completely every frame. So I stopped watching and counted pixels instead:

```text
[*] 55 frames of (128, 256)

=== pixel-value histogram over the whole video ===
   value   0 : 1799436
   value 252 : 2804
   ... 2 distinct values, max 252
```

Two pixel values in the entire video, and 51 lit pixels in every frame but one. That rules
out the whole blob-detection reflex straight away: there is no brightness gradient, no size
difference, no color separation. Every lit pixel is byte-identical to every other one, so the
signal cannot be distinguished by looking at any single pixel. It has to be distinguished by where
it is.

The 2804 is worth a second of attention, because 55 x 51 is 2805. Frame 23 is the one exception with
50, so two of its points landed on the same pixel. Nothing in the frame says which two.

Two more numbers set up the hypothesis. The frame is 256 wide, which is exactly the range of a
byte, and there are 55 frames. One character per frame, carried in an x coordinate.

### The tracker that did not work

The obvious first move is that 50 of the 51 points are re-randomized noise and one is a beam that
moves smoothly, so a greedy nearest-neighbor chain should follow it. I ran all 51 points of frame 0
as starting seeds and scored each chain by its mean step:

```text
=== best chains by mean step ===
  start  24: mean step  10.45  max  22.36  first pts [(161, 69), (164, 70), (151, 66), (159, 61)]
  start   0: mean step  10.93  max  24.41
  start  34: mean step  11.88  max  27.59

  x as ascii : .......................................................
```

Not one printable character out of 55. The point density explains it. Measured on the video, the
nearest point in the next frame is 13.3px away on average, and the beam, once I knew where it was,
turns out to move 16.5px per frame and as much as 54.9px. So the true beam is usually not the
closest point, and a single wrong hop drops the chain into the noise for good. The winning chain's
10.45px mean step is not evidence of a track either: all 51 chains scored between 10.45px and
15.07px, so the best one is just the luckiest random walk.

That dead end is the useful part of this challenge, because it says exactly what the solution needs
to be. Local continuity is not a strong enough discriminator at this noise density. The beam has to
be found by a property that holds across the whole video and that uniform noise cannot imitate.

### The crib

The flag format was `flag{ECSC2026_...}`, so before writing another tracker I tested the encoding
directly. If frame i carries `msg[i]` in a coordinate, then frame i must contain a point at that
coordinate. That is a one-line check and it needs no tracking at all:

```text
=== does frame i contain a point with x == ord(crib[i]) ? ===
  f 0 'f' (102): x-hits [(102, 64)]
  f 1 'l' (108): x-hits [(108, 79)]
  f 2 'a' ( 97): x-hits [(97, 50), (97, 90)]
  f 3 'g' (103): x-hits [(103, 95)]
  f 4 '{' (123): x-hits [(123, 93)]
  f 5 'E' ( 69): x-hits [(69, 83)]
  f 6 'C' ( 67): x-hits [(67, 26), (67, 68)]
  f 7 'S' ( 83): x-hits [(83, 52)]
  f 8 'C' ( 67): x-hits [(67, 39)]
  f 9 '2' ( 50): x-hits [(50, 32)]
  f10 '0' ( 48): x-hits [(48, 33)]
  f11 '2' ( 50): x-hits [(50, 41)]
  f12 '6' ( 54): x-hits [(54, 55)]
```

Thirteen out of thirteen on x. The same test against y produced a single hit across all thirteen
frames, and that one was a coincidence. So x is the byte.

The y column of those hits is where the challenge actually lives:

```text
64, 79, 90, 95, 93, 83, 68, 52, 39, 32, 33, 41, 55
```

It climbs, turns over near 95, falls to 32 and starts climbing again. That is a sine, that is the
"oscillating beam" from the statement, and it is the global property the nearest-neighbor tracker
had no way to use.

### Fitting the oscillation

Seed the y track from the crib, resolving the two ambiguous frames (2 and 6, which each had two
x-hits) by picking the candidate closest to the local trend, then least-squares fit
`y(t) = C + A·sin(wt) + B·cos(wt)` while sweeping the frequency:

```python
t = np.arange(len(seed_y), dtype=float)
best = None
for w in np.linspace(0.05, 1.5, 30000):
    M = np.stack([np.ones_like(t), np.sin(w * t), np.cos(w * t)], 1)
    coef, res, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(M, seed_y, rcond=None)
    err = np.sum((M @ coef - seed_y) ** 2)
    if best is None or err < best[0]:
        best = (err, w, coef)
```

```text
[+] crib y-track: [64, 79, 90, 95, 93, 83, 68, 52, 39, 32, 33, 41, 55]
[+] fit: w=0.50063 (period 12.55 frames)  C=63.5 A=32.0 B=0.0  sse=1.16
```

The model has four free parameters, not three: C, A and B come out of the least squares, and w is
fitted as well by the 30000-point sweep. Four parameters against 13 points means a low error proves
little on its own. What does is where the parameters landed. C = 63.5 is the vertical center of a
128px frame, A = 32.0 is exactly a quarter of the height, and B = 0.0 means the beam starts at
phase zero. Those are authored constants, and the whole 13-point track sits inside a summed squared
error of 1.16.

Extrapolating the curve to all 55 frames and taking the nearest point in each gets most of the way:

```text
[*] max |y - fit| over all frames: 1.15

MESSAGE: flag{ECSC2026_vector_whi?pers_transmis?}on_in?erce(ned}
```

That 1.15 is the residual of the point the script picked, not of the beam, and in six frames it
picked a noise pixel instead: 24, 38, 39, 45, 50 and 51, which are exactly the six corrupted
characters. Scored against the real beam, the worst residual in the video is 1.32px, at frame 50.
So the corrupted characters are not tracking failures, they are frames where a noise pixel happened
to land closer to the curve than the beam did.

### Resolving the collisions

So instead of taking the single nearest point, I listed every candidate within 1.6px of the curve
per frame, a band wide enough to hold the beam in all 55 frames, and looked at how constrained the
reading was:

```text
[*] frames with a unique candidate: 32/55

  f 0 y*= 63.56  x candidates [102, 108] -> ['f', 'l']
  f36 y*= 39.99  x candidates [105, 112] -> ['i', 'p']
  f51 y*= 76.01  x candidates [110, 116] -> ['n', 't']
  f53 y*= 95.11  x candidates [100, 102] -> ['d', 'f']
```

32 of the 55 frames resolve uniquely with no help at all, and no frame carries more than five
candidates, so the ambiguity is narrow everywhere it exists. The reading is 55 characters for 55
frames, and the last check is that every frame contains the expected character sitting on the curve:

```text
[*] hypothesis (55 chars vs 55 frames)
[+] every frame contains the expected character on the curve: True
```

Flag: `flag{ECSC2026_vector_whispers_transmission_intercepted}`

## Takeaway

The technical lesson is about how you separate signal from noise when the two are physically
identical. Every lit pixel here had the same value, the same size and the same shape, so no test on
a single pixel could work. Proximity did not work either: the beam moves further between frames than
the distance to the nearest noise point, which is why the greedy chain never held. What separated
them was that the beam obeyed a four-parameter model across all 55 frames while the noise obeyed
nothing. Once I had a model I was not asking "which point is nearest" any more, I was asking "which
point is consistent with the whole video", and that question the noise cannot answer. This is matched
filtering in the shape of a puzzle: correlate against the expected structure rather than chase the
strongest local return. Radar, GPS and DSSS all pull signals out from under the noise floor the same
way.

The known-plaintext crib deserves its own credit. I spent time building a tracker before testing
whether I even knew what a "character" looked like in this encoding, when the flag prefix let me
confirm the encoding, rule out y, and hand me the seed track for the fit, all from one loop with no
tracker involved. When you know part of the answer, test the encoding hypothesis first and build the
extraction second.

On the defensive side this is an optical covert channel, and it is worth being clear about why it
was recoverable. The designer randomized the cover but not the carrier. The 50 decoy points were
re-drawn every frame while the beam followed one fixed, deterministic, low-frequency sinusoid for
the entire transmission, so the carrier stood out as the only structured thing in the video. A real
exfiltration channel built this way would randomize the carrier too, keying the trajectory from a
shared secret so that the receiver knows where to look and an interceptor sees 51 indistinguishable
random points. That is the difference between hiding data and encrypting it, and the same lesson
applies to any covert channel: obscurity in the modulation is not confidentiality.
